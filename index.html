Why this exists

AI chatbots and agents are probabilistic: ask the same question twice and you can get two different answers. So when an AI promises a refund, quotes a price or takes an action, the most reliable account of what happened is a record made at that moment, kept where nobody can quietly change it, and checkable by someone other than the company that made it. Without one, a dispute comes down to your word against a log only the other side controls.

The same holds if you run an AI model yourself, on a laptop, a home server or a small board. If what your model wrote or did is ever disputed, a record made at the time, sealed by an outside clock, is far stronger evidence than a log you could have edited later.

The law is moving the same way. Under the EU's new Product Liability Directive, which covers software including AI placed on the market or put into service after 9 December 2026, injured people do not have to prove fault (they still have to prove the product was defective, the damage, and the link between them), a court can order evidence to be disclosed, and failing to disclose it can lead the court to presume the product was defective. It is mainly about products supplied in the course of business. This page does not say whether it could reach someone running a model only for themselves; note that its definition of manufacturer includes a person who develops or produces a product for their own use (Article 4(10)).

So ask two questions: can I have the record? And can someone other than you check it? This page shows how the first check works.

    Handed an operator's AI records? Three questions to ask

    For regulators, supervisors, courts, insurers, auditors and anyone else who receives AI records from the company that made them. This page calls that company the “operator”; under the EU AI Act it is most often the “deployer”.

    1. Is it complete for the scope the operator declared? Ask for the declaration first: what is recorded, how often, and what happens when recording fails (C1), and which gaps were declared in advance. The draft tests report every declared gap; whether one can ever count as a pass is a point still open for comment.
    2. Who else holds a copy or a count? A record checked only against itself cannot show a full rewrite or a consistent under-count. Look for checkpoints stamped by an outside clock and witnessed by others (C6), and counts from someone the operator does not control, such as the AI provider's own bill (C14).
    3. If an entry were missing or changed, how would anyone notice? Numbering and links (C3) show a deletion, as the demonstration above does. Counters (C14) show entries dropped before they were numbered. A heartbeat (C22) shows when recording was off.

    Draft test vectors for two of the tests, T3 and T14a, show each case, including two where a check passes although something went wrong and one where it can only hold, because only an outside copy or count could catch them. They come with a plain-language index for supervisors (REGULATOR_QUESTIONS.txt) at github.com/StSForensics/o4m-record-tests. They are self-run. One second reader, run for the author by a family member, wrote a separate checker whose verdicts matched on 31 of 50 inputs; nobody else has reproduced the results. This is a reading aid, not guidance from any regulator and not legal advice.

    Running your own model? Keep a record that others can check

    A record you keep about yourself is worth only as much as someone else can check. These steps make it checkable. They cost nothing. You need recording software for steps 1 to 4. StS does not make one.

    1. For every answer, keep: what you asked and what came back (on your own machine), a fingerprint of each, the fingerprint of the model file (use the box above), the software and its version, and the settings (temperature, seed). A fingerprint alone cannot show what was said.
    2. Seal it with an outside clock, regularly. Send only the fingerprint to a free timestamp service (RFC 3161) or a public log, and keep the receipt it sends back; without it the seal cannot be checked. Your content never leaves your machine. The seal shows the record existed by that time, so nobody, you included, can backdate it or change it afterwards without it showing. It cannot show you recorded everything; step 3 covers that.
    3. Record all the time, or say when you didn't. A gap you cannot explain weakens everything else.
    4. If your AI acts for you (sends an email, posts, buys), seal the action before it runs.
    5. Once a dispute looks likely, delete nothing. Deleting relevant records at that point can count against you in court.
    6. On a phone, other apps usually cannot see inside the AI app, so the record depends on what that app saves. If the model runs on the phone itself, its maker may hold nothing. Check whether the app can export its history, export regularly, and fingerprint and seal each export (steps 1 and 2).

    Records cut both ways: they show what actually happened, including your own prompts. They protect people who acted honestly. This is not legal advice.

    What a record can prove, one step at a time

    1. 1
      Unchanged since it was fingerprinted

      Needs: a SHA-256 fingerprint you trust. Checkable here

    2. 2
      Existed by a certain time

      Needs: the fingerprint stamped by an independent timestamp authority (RFC 3161, or an EU qualified timestamp). Coming next

    3. 3
      Came from a known party

      Needs: a digital signature checked against that party's published key. Coming next

    4. 4
      Nobody quietly rewrote the register

      Needs: the record listed in a public transparency log that many parties compare. Coming next

    5. 5
      Nothing was left out

      Needs: independent counts, such as the AI provider's own bill, plus a statement of when capture was live. On your own machine there is no provider bill. A recorder heartbeat and a declared list of gaps are the best substitute, but they cannot show you never ran the model without the recorder. Needs the operator

    6. 6
      What it says actually happened

      Needs: a record from someone else, such as your bank statement showing the refund. An intact record of a false statement is still false. For your own agent: the recipient's copy of an email it sent, or your bank's record of a payment. Partly yours to check

    Background, and where this fits

    I have published an open comparison of UK and EU rules on this (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23546436). Outside financial services, none of the binding rules I reviewed clearly requires AI logs to be kept so that someone other than the party that produced them can check them for alteration or omission. On 1 November 2026 I published open tests for exactly that property, as a public comment draft open until 1 December 2026 (doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.23857888).

    Does this compete with SCITT, AIUC-1 or other standards?

    No, and it is not meant to. These tests and this page do not define a log format, a standard or a certificate. This page makes no records, and StS Forensics does not supply the systems it checks. The tests ask one extra question of work that already exists: could someone outside the operator tell if anything was left out or changed?

    • SCITT (the IETF architecture in RFC 9943) is a way to register signed statements in a transparency service. The tests sit beside it. RFC 9943 itself notes, in section 9.3, that issuers can submit some statements and not others, which is the gap the tests focus on.
    • AIUC-1 is a standard for AI agents that is updated every quarter; its Q4 2026 release (15 October 2026) added mandatory third-party testing of data and privacy risk and of accountability risk. The tests could give a tester something concrete to check about records. They are not part of AIUC-1, and there is no affiliation or endorsement either way.
    • Other standards, for example from ETSI, CEN-CENELEC and ISO/IEC, are separate bodies of work. Where they ask for logs, the tests ask whether an outsider could check those logs for omission as well as alteration.

    A record can follow every one of these and still fail the tests if nothing outside the operator can check it for omission. Corrections and comments are welcome until 1 December 2026: comments@sts-forensics.com, subject "O4M Record Tests".
